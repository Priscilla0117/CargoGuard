## The story and the points to land

The earlier version listed functions but gave too little attention to why an employee would use them. This version follows the work and makes the practical consequence explicit. Keep the exact actions below; the change is how you explain them.

- **Opening:** ask whether this draft needs correction. Pause for one beat, then point to the two container values. Do not start with a long greeting or a list of technologies.
- **Classification:** different email requests need different handling. Show a real model run; do not imply the app fulfils invoice requests or creates shipping instructions.
- **Extraction:** differently named headings can describe the same field. Point to the original headings, then the structured row, so the viewer sees what the parser achieved.
- **Discrepancy:** the employee can identify the particular values that need checking and use the evidence in a follow-up.
- **Human review:** missing or unreadable evidence leaves work unfinished. Explain the next action without treating review status as a software failure.
- **Chat:** imagine a colleague taking over this same selected case. Show a useful answer and one opened reference; do not read the entire response aloud. The assistant does not automatically understand every case or replace human review.
- **Source selection:** make clear which draft the saved result covers. Pause on the excluded-attachment notice so an all-match outcome does not imply every attachment was checked.
- **Linked preview:** say “watch this preview,” make the temporary edit, then pause for two seconds on both changed checks. Explain the effect before restoring the real source value and saving the confirmation.
- **History:** let a colleague follow the earlier result and the recorded review. Do not imply a self-entered reviewer name is authenticated corporate identity.
- **Architecture and tests:** explain responsibilities and show dated evidence. Avoid reading every service name, test count or limitation from a crowded slide.
- **Close:** briefly return to the original five-versus-four case. The payoff is that the employee can explain the discrepancy and prepare the follow-up. Do not show this unchanged case as resolved.

The strongest moments are the evidence link, the explicit draft/exclusion choice and the linked-field preview with saved history. Present them as useful design choices. A chatbot alone does not establish uniqueness, and no available comparison establishes that other teams lack these features.

### Speaking and editing

Speak as if explaining the screen to a colleague beside you. Stress a few useful words, such as “five,” “four,” “which draft” and “before I save.” Keep an interested, conversational tone. Do not manufacture urgency, act surprised at rehearsed results, or announce that a feature is amazing.

Use narration over genuine screen footage rather than silently performing every navigation step before explaining it. Keep the important action and its real result visible. You may trim dead time with truthful wait/cache labels, but do not stitch a failed action to an unrelated success. Only rehearse, time and verify the final edit before calling it under five minutes.

If the take runs long, cut repeated cursor movement, the optional container normalization detail, or lengthy response scrolling first. Retain the core proof, the source-selection boundary, the restored source value before save, the development-data qualification and the cloud/future-work distinction.
